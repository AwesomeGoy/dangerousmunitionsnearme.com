What is PFAS?

PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are a large group of human-made chemicals used for decades in nonstick, stain-resistant and waterproof products and in some firefighting foams. They break down very slowly, so they build up in water, soil, fish, and people.

This page summarizes official public guidance. It is not medical or legal advice and it does not say whether any place is safe or unsafe. For health questions, talk to a clinician or your local health department. In an emergency, call 911 and follow local officials.

Why military bases?

For years the military used firefighting foam that contained PFAS in training and emergencies. The Department of Defense says it is assessing PFAS use or possible releases at 727 installations, National Guard facilities, closed bases (BRAC) and Formerly Used Defense Sites, as of 2026-06-30. A listing means DoD is investigating. It does not by itself mean your drinking water is contaminated.

What EPA says is known

  • PFAS are widely used and long lasting, and many are found in the blood of people and animals all over the world.
  • Studies show exposure to some PFAS may be linked to harmful health effects in people and animals.
  • Only a small amount gets in through skin, so EPA says showering, bathing and washing dishes in water with PFAS are unlikely to significantly raise your risk. Drinking water and food matter more.

What you can do

  1. On city or public water: ask your water utility whether it has tested for PFAS and for the results. Your yearly Consumer Confidence Report may list them.
  2. On a private well: have it tested by a state-certified lab using EPA methods. Your state health or environmental agency can give you a list of labs.
  3. If levels concern you: ask your state health or environmental agency what it recommends. Consider a home filter certified to lower PFAS, and use another water source for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth and baby formula until you know more.
  4. Fish: follow state fish advisories for waters affected by PFAS.
  5. Health questions: talk to your doctor. ATSDR (part of CDC) has PFAS information for patients and clinicians.
  6. Near a base: check the installation's PFAS or Restoration Advisory Board page and DoD's off-base drinking water information.
