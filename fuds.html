Formerly Used Defense Sites (FUDS)

Formerly Used Defense Sites are places the military once owned, leased or used, and that were transferred out of Defense Department control before October 17, 1986. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers runs the FUDS program for the Department of Defense, investigating and cleaning up contamination the military left behind.

This page summarizes official public guidance. It is not medical or legal advice and it does not say whether any place is safe or unsafe. For health questions, talk to a clinician or your local health department. In an emergency, call 911 and follow local officials.

What is shown here

All 10,150 FUDS properties in the Corps’ FUDS GIS (FY24), checked field by field on Nov 25, 2026. They fall into three groups:

  • FUDS munitions 1,493 properties with a munitions response project (Military Munitions Response Program). Old munitions may still be in the ground. The Corps publishes 1,685 munitions response site (MRS) boundaries for 984 of them; an MRS is a specific area, such as a bombing range or practice target.
  • Other FUDS 1,385 properties with other cleanup projects only: hazardous, toxic or radioactive waste (HTRW), containerized waste such as old tanks, drums or pipelines, or building demolition and debris removal. (A few are marked as having projects but have no project details in the FY24 data.)
  • Other FUDS 7,272 properties with no project (for example, the Corps found nothing that needed a response, or the property was not eligible).

8,847 properties have an official map point. 1,303 have no usable official point and are listed only. Two are listed with a caution because their official point falls in a different state from the one listed. Some munitions ranges are very large, so a boundary can be tens of miles from the property point. Boundaries are simplified for a fast website and are not survey-grade.

What the MRSPP number means

DoD ranks each munitions response site with the Munitions Response Site Prioritization Protocol (MRSPP). It looks at explosive hazards, chemical warfare materiel and munitions chemicals, and gives a relative priority from 1 (highest potential hazard) to 8 (lowest). It sets the order in which sites get attention; it is not a measure of risk to a particular person, and a high number does not mean no action is needed.

Sites that cannot get a number get one of three other ratings: evaluation pending (not enough information yet), no longer required (the response is done and only long-term management remains) or no known or suspected hazard. The Corps publishes these as codes 09, 10 and 11; they are shown here in that order, which matches how they line up with project status in the Corps’ data.

If you find something that could be a munition

Follow DoD’s 3Rs of explosives safety: Recognize that it may be a munition, Retreat without touching or moving it, and Report it by calling 911.
