Nuclear legacy sites

This page covers two kinds of places left over from the U.S. nuclear weapons and uranium era: 108 sites the Department of Energy’s Office of Legacy Management (LM) looks after, and 523 abandoned uranium mines on and near the Navajo Nation.

This page summarizes official public guidance. It is not medical or legal advice and it does not say whether any place is safe or unsafe. For health questions, talk to a clinician or your local health department. In an emergency, call 911 and follow local officials.

DOE Legacy Management sites

When cleanup at a uranium mill, a Manhattan Project or Atomic Energy Commission site, or a former nuclear test site is finished, DOE’s Office of Legacy Management takes over long-term care: inspections, groundwater monitoring, keeping disposal cells and records, and limits on how the land is used. Being on this list means DOE has ongoing responsibility; it does not by itself mean a place is unsafe today. Each site links to DOE’s own page and fact sheet.

108 sites (73 DOE sites plus 35 FUSRAP sites already handed over to DOE). These are separate from the 20 active FUSRAP sites the Army Corps of Engineers is still cleaning up. One entry, the Plowshare/Vela Uniform Records, CO, Site, is a records site in Grand Junction, Colorado, not a contamination site, so it is not used in distances or alerts.

Abandoned uranium mines on the Navajo Nation

From 1944 to 1986, nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore were mined from Navajo lands under leases with the Navajo Nation. Many Navajo people worked the mines and lived near them with their families. The mines are closed, but uranium contamination remains at the mines and at some homes and water sources. EPA works with the Navajo Nation Environmental Protection Agency (NNEPA) on assessment and cleanup; EPA reports more than $1.7 billion in enforcement agreements and settlements, which make funding available to begin assessment and cleanup at 230 of the 523 mines. EPA and NNEPA named 46 priority mines based on radiation levels, how close they are to homes, and the potential for water contamination.

These mines are on Navajo Nation land. This page lists them only to point people to the official programs; for local information, contact NNEPA or EPA’s Navajo Nation abandoned uranium mines program.

EPA’s inventory covers mines on or within one mile of the Navajo Nation, so 19 of the mapped mines sit just outside the boundary. By map point, 347 are in Arizona, 151 in New Mexico and 25 in Utah.

Health guidance from EPA

EPA says people can come in contact with uranium and radiation by spending time at abandoned uranium mines, living in a home built with material from a mine or mill site, drinking unregulated water, or not following good water-hauling practices. EPA lists possible health effects as lung cancer from breathing radioactive particles, and bone cancer and kidney damage from radionuclides in drinking water. NNEPA policy prohibits drinking from unregulated water sources such as livestock (windmill) wells and springs. Read EPA’s health effects of uranium page for how to avoid contact and how to haul and store drinking water safely.
